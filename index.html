River current, finishing time and pace

As GlommaDyppen is swum downstream, the river flow on race day has a major impact on finishing times.

The flow in the Glomma has varied from roughly 300 to 1300 m³/s between 2016 and 2026. It can reduce finishing times by approximately 10% to 45% compared with still-water swimming.

Swimmer speed matters — slower swimmers benefit more from the current than faster swimmers, because the current speed is a larger share of a slower swimmer's speed. For 11 km: a fast swimmer (1:20/100 m) gets 10% to 35% (at 300 and 1300 m³/s), while a 2:10/100 m swimmer gets 17% to 46%.

Drag anywhere on the dial to set the direction the wind blows from; the slider sets its strength. The barb follows the usual convention of pointing into the wind, but is scaled for this range: each full feather is 2 m/s and each half feather 1 m/s. Switching wind on selects a different model, not a correction bolted onto the first. The flow-only model has a steeper current slope because it quietly absorbs whatever wind those editions happened to have; the flow-plus-wind model fits both at once, so its current slope drops and the wind is charged explicitly. The two answer different questions and will not always agree. The wind term is squared, since doubling the chop costs far more than twice the speed, and its size is fitted separately for each race and field reference — winners are consistently less wind-sensitive than the median swimmer. It rests on nine race days with one strong headwind, so 2026 carries a lot of weight. What actually slows you is the chop the wind raises, not air drag, so the effect is uneven along the course and rises sharply once waves build.

Data set

There are two models available for the pace with and without current. One is based on the 2022 edition, when GlommaDyppen was moved to Heia Lake. The other ignores the 2022 edition and extrapolates the no-current pace from the trend line across all Glomma editions.
▸ How accurate is this?

Each edition was held out in turn, the model refitted on the remaining ones, and that edition then predicted. Flow only uses current alone; flow + wind also feeds in the wind that edition actually had. Comparing the columns shows where knowing the wind genuinely pays.

Method

Each edition is reduced to one ground speed v for the chosen field reference (winner, mean of the top ten, median, or last finisher), paired with the mean river discharge Q in m³/s and the race-day wind. Two models are fitted by ordinary least squares:

A   v = a + b·Q
B   v = a + b·Q − k·W    where   W = e·|e|   and   e = U·cos(θ − φ)

U is wind speed, θ the direction it blows from, and φ the course bearing (2 km 240°, 6 km 216°, 11 km 228°), so e is the head component in m/s and is negative for a tailwind. W squares it while keeping the sign, because velocity loss rises faster than linearly with wave height. b and k are estimated jointly, so model B's current slope is lower than model A's: model A has no wind term and its slope absorbs whatever wind those editions happened to carry. Under the lake data set the intercept a is pinned to the still-water 2022 edition; under extrapolated it is fitted freely. The 2022 lake edition is otherwise excluded, as it was swum on still water at a different venue.

Accuracy is assessed by leave-one-edition-out cross-validation: each edition is withheld, both models are refitted on the remainder, and the withheld edition predicted. Error is (actual ÷ predicted − 1), expressed as a percentage of finishing time. The table gives the median absolute error with the 10th–90th percentile of the signed error in brackets.

Limitations. n = 9 editions (7 for the 6 km), and only two or three of them had a headwind, so k rests heavily on 2026 and its confidence interval is wide; the 11 km last-finisher estimate even comes out negative, which is physically impossible and marks the limit of what this sample can resolve. Head and tail winds are constrained to a single coefficient. Allowing them separate coefficients is well motivated — a headwind disrupts breathing in a way a tailwind does not, and on a downstream course a headwind always opposes the current, which steepens the chop — but with so few headwind editions the split is not identifiable here: it worsens cross-validated error from 5.3% to 7.9% and returns unstable, sometimes negative coefficients. Symmetry is therefore an admission of insufficient data, not evidence that the effect is symmetric. Wind figures are daily means rather than race-window measurements, and gusts and wind shifts during a long swim are not represented at all.

Wind helps the 2 km and 6 km and still hurts the 11 km. The 2 km is swum in one wind, in one direction, in twenty minutes, which is where the correction bites hardest — it cuts the winner error from 8.1% to 4.3%. Over two and a half hours the wind shifts and the course turns, so one race-day average describes an 11 km swim poorly, and the plain flow model is already good to 2-3% there. Treat the widest bands, especially the 2 km last finisher, as a warning that a single individual's time is barely predictable at all.

The course

All three races swim downstream in the Glomma, from Bingsfoss near Blaker south-west to a shared finish at Fetsund Lenser, where the river opens into Nordre Øyeren. The 6 km and 2 km races start further down the same river, so their start lines sit at the 5 km and 9 km marks of the 11 km course. The overall heading is about 228°, which is what the wind model uses to split a wind into head, tail and cross components.
